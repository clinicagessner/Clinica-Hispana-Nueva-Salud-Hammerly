It's two in the morning, and from the kids' room comes the same dry cough as last week. Mateo, who is six, sits up in bed, coughs four or five times in a row and lies back down. During the day he's fine: he plays soccer at recess and eats like always. But when he runs hard or catches a cold, his mom can hear a high-pitched sound at the end of each breath, like a tiny whistle coming from his chest.

Plenty of Spring Branch families know that scene and explain it a dozen ways: "it's his throat," "it's the cold weather," "the cough from his last cold never left." Sometimes that's all it is. Other times, the pairing of a nighttime cough and a whistle in the chest is how asthma shows up in a child. This article helps you tell one from the other and know what to do in each case.

## The urgent part first: when it's an emergency

If your child has any of these signs, **call 911** or go straight to an emergency room, without waiting for morning:

- The **ribs sink in**, or the skin between them and below the neck pulls in, with every breath.
- The **lips, tongue or fingernails look blue or gray**.
- They **can't talk**, or only get out two or three words before needing another breath.
- Breathing is very fast, the nostrils flare wide or the belly pumps hard to pull air in.
- They're very drowsy, confused or hard to wake.
- The rescue medicine already prescribed for them was used and there's no improvement within a few minutes.

At that point, loading your child into the car to look for a clinic isn't the move: the ambulance carries oxygen and starts helping the moment it arrives.

## Why the cough gets worse at night

In the early morning hours, the body makes less of the substances that keep the airways open, and lying down lets mucus from the nose drip toward the throat. In a child with sensitive airways, that's enough for the cough to show up at nearly the same hour every night. That's why one of the most useful questions to have answered before the visit is: **how many nights a week does the cough wake them up?**

The whistle, called wheezing in medical terms, is the sound of air moving through tubes that have narrowed. You hear it best when they breathe out. It isn't the same as snoring or the "snotty" rattle you hear in a stuffed-up baby's nose; if you put your ear to your child's chest, a wheeze sounds like a soft whistle coming from inside.

## Clues that point toward asthma

A common cold can leave a cough behind for two or three weeks, and that alone doesn't mean asthma. What stands out is the pattern. According to the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/asthma/), asthma is one of the most common chronic conditions in childhood, and these clues tend to repeat:

- The cough comes back **every time** they catch a cold and lasts longer than it does for their siblings.
- They cough or wheeze when running, laughing hard or crying.
- Symptoms get worse at night or at dawn.
- Someone in the family has asthma, allergies or eczema.
- The child has dry, itchy patches of skin or a stuffy nose much of the year.
- Between flare-ups, they seem completely healthy.

One clue on its own proves nothing; three or four at once are worth having the clinic's medical team listen to those lungs.

## What triggers the cough in a Houston fall

November brings Houston its first cold fronts, and several triggers arrive along with them:

| Trigger | How you notice it | What you can do at home |
|---|---|---|
| Colds and bugs from school | Cough and wheeze start two or three days after the runny nose | Handwashing as soon as they get home and a flu shot for the family |
| Sudden temperature swings | Coughing when stepping from a heated room into the chilly morning air | A scarf over nose and mouth on crisp mornings |
| Cigarette, vape or wood smoke | The cough follows family gatherings or backyard fire pits | Nobody smokes inside the house or car, not even with the window down |
| Dust, mold and pets | Worse the first time the heater kicks on or when sleeping with the dog | Change the air filter and wash bedding in hot water |
| Fall pollen | Itchy nose and eyes along with the cough | Keep windows shut on high-pollen days and bathe them before bed |

Smoke deserves its own note. Even when the smoker steps out to the patio, the smell that clings to clothes and hair still irritates a child's airways.

## What to watch for and write down at home

Arriving with specific details helps far more than saying "he coughs a lot." For one or two weeks, jot down on your phone:

1. **What time the cough hits** and how many nights it wakes them.
2. **Whether you heard the wheeze** and when: running, laughing, asleep or during a cold.
3. **What happened right before**: cold air, smoke, dust, a sleepover at a house with a cat.
4. **Whether they missed school** or stopped playing because they tired out fast.
5. **A short video** of the cough or of their breathing at night. Kids often stop coughing the minute they walk into the clinic, and the video speaks for them.

## What the clinic's medical team does

During a [respiratory illness visit](/en/services/enfermedades-respiratorias), the clinic's medical team checks your child's vital signs, listens to their lungs and looks at the nose and throat. If there's a fever or it all started as a cold, the team may order a rapid flu and COVID test, with results in a few minutes, to see whether a virus is behind the flare-up.

When the cough comes with sneezing, itchy eyes or hives, an [allergy evaluation](/en/services/alergias) helps pin down what sets it off inside and outside the house. With all that information, the team explains what they think is going on, which treatment makes sense and how to spot a flare-up early.

When the plan includes medicine for your child's chest, the prescription is sent electronically to the pharmacy you choose (that's how [e-prescriptions](/en/services/farmacia) work here). Before you leave, ask how and when to use it, and if your child needs an inhaler, practice the technique in front of the team: often the problem isn't the medicine but how it reaches the lungs.

## Asthma, school and sports

A child with well-controlled asthma can run, swim and play on the school team. If a [school or sports physical](/en/services/examen-fisico-escolar) is coming up this year, it's a good moment to bring up the night cough or the wheezing during runs, even if your child is fine that day. Also ask whether the school needs a form about their breathing condition, and give the school nurse and the coach a heads-up.

## Myths we still hear

- **"If it whistles, there's phlegm in the chest."** Not always. The wheeze comes from tightened airways, and cough syrups don't open them.
- **"Kids outgrow asthma on their own."** Some children improve with age, but there's no way to know ahead of time, and meanwhile they need to sleep and breathe well.
- **"Inhalers make you dependent."** Used the way the medical team directs, they're the most direct way to deliver medicine right where it's needed.

## When to bring them in

You don't have to wait for a flare-up. Bring your child to Hammerly Blvd if the cough wakes them more than one night a week, if the wheeze returns with every cold or if a cold's cough is still hanging on after three weeks. Walking in is fine, so is calling ahead for a time, and families without insurance pay directly. What helps us most that day: your notes from those nights, the cough video and whatever syrups or remedies you've already tried.
