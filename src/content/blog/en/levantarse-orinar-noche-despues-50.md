It's 3 a.m. and you're tiptoeing down a dark hallway to the bathroom again, trying not to wake anyone. You get back into bed, lie there a while before sleep returns, and at five it happens all over again. Plenty of men past 50 live like this for years. But waking up to urinate has a name, nocturia, and it nearly always has a cause worth tracking down.

## How many trips count as a problem?

Getting up once a night, especially after 60, happens to a lot of healthy people. When it's two, three or more trips almost every night, you feel it the next day: drowsy behind the wheel and slower at work. If it has already changed the way you sleep, it deserves a closer look.

## The key question: a lot of urine or just a little?

Before blaming your prostate, notice how much comes out on each nighttime trip. That one detail points down two different roads:

- **If you pass a good amount every time**, your body is making more urine at night than it should. That points to evening drinks, high blood sugar, legs that swell during the day or a sleep problem.
- **If only a little comes out even though the urge woke you**, the issue sits more in the bladder or the outflow: an enlarged prostate, an irritable bladder or an infection.

Many men have a bit of both, which is why measuring beats guessing.

## What's often behind it after 50

**A prostate that grows with age.** Benign prostate enlargement is very common at this stage of life. As it squeezes the tube urine travels through, the bladder doesn't empty completely and fills up again fast. It tends to come with a weaker stream or dribbling at the end.

**What you drink after the evening meal.** The six o'clock coffee, the soda with dinner, the beers during the game or the hot tea at bedtime all reach your bladder right when you're lying down. Alcohol and caffeine also make the kidneys let go of more water.

**High blood sugar.** When glucose climbs, the body tries to flush it out in the urine and pulls water along with it. Peeing a lot and feeling thirsty all the time can be the first hint of diabetes nobody has measured yet.

**Blood pressure and some medications.** Certain blood pressure or swelling pills make you urinate more, and when they're taken late, that effect lands in the middle of the night. Don't change them on your own: timing or the plan gets adjusted at the visit.

**Swollen legs.** If your shoes feel tight by evening or your socks leave a mark, fluid has pooled in your legs from long hours standing or sitting. Once you lie down it moves back into circulation, the kidneys filter it and it wakes you up as urine.

**Loud snoring and pauses in breathing.** Sleep apnea, those breathing pauses a partner usually notices first, also raises nighttime urine. If you snore heavily or doze off in any armchair during the day, mention it.

**A urinary infection.** If burning, sudden urges or a different smell come along with the trips, it may be an infection, and in men that always deserves a check.

## Your night diary: three days that say a lot

The most useful thing you can bring to the visit fits on one sheet of paper. For three days in a row, write down when you drink and how much, when you urinate and, if you can, how much comes out. A kitchen measuring cup set aside just for this does the job. A well-filled page looks like this:

| Time | What you drank and how much | How much urine | Did the urge wake you |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6:30 p.m. | Coffee with milk, one cup | — | — |
| 8:00 p.m. | Water with dinner, two glasses | Half a cup | — |
| 10:45 p.m. | — | One cup | No, before bed |
| 2:10 a.m. | — | A cup and a half | Yes |
| 4:30 a.m. | — | Half a cup | Yes |
| 6:15 a.m. | — | One cup | On getting up |

With that page, the medical team can work out whether more than a third of your whole day's urine is being made at night, which points to causes other than the prostate.

## Changes at home that make a real difference

While you wait for the visit, these adjustments help many men:

1. **Shift your water toward the morning.** The goal isn't to drink less overall, but to drink well during the day and ease off fluids in the two or three hours before bed.
2. **Set a cutoff for coffee and alcohol.** After mid-afternoon, skip the caffeine; the nighttime beer usually sends its bill at 2 a.m.
3. **Put your legs up for a while in the afternoon.** If they swell, lying down with your feet raised for an hour before dinner lets that fluid leave before you go to bed.
4. **Urinate twice before lying down.** Go, wait a few minutes and try again; that way your bladder starts the night emptier.

## When it's not for the clinic but for 911

Some nighttime trips to the bathroom turn into something that can't wait for sunrise. **Dial 911 or head to an emergency room** in these situations:

- Your bladder feels full and sore, you push and no urine comes out at all.
- On top of the bathroom trips, a high fever with shaking chills sets in and it burns to urinate.
- You wake up short of breath while lying flat, have to sit up to breathe and your legs are very swollen.
- You feel pain or pressure in your chest, even if you think it's just from a bad night.
- A very intense, sudden pain hits one testicle.

Blood in your urine, even without pain, isn't something to let slide either: come in to get checked as soon as you notice it.
