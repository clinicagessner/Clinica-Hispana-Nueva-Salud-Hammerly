Mention a burning feeling when you pee in a lot of Hispanic households and the advice shows up long before anyone books a visit: drink cranberry juice, down a couple of liters of water, brew some horsetail tea, stir baking soda into a glass. It comes with love, and a few of those tips aren't entirely wrong. Trouble starts when the remedy becomes a way to cover up an infection that keeps moving.

Here the clinic's medical team sorts out which kitchen remedies can genuinely support you and which ones just cost you time, so you can tell when a home remedy is a reasonable sidekick and when it's time to get checked.

## The one idea to keep in mind

**Preventing** an infection and **treating** one are two very different jobs. Cranberry and water can be part of prevention, especially for people whose infections keep coming back. But once bacteria have settled into the bladder and the symptoms are obvious, no juice or tea reliably gets rid of them. At that point you need to confirm what's going on and, if it is an infection, get treatment prescribed during a visit.

## Cranberry: what we know and what we don't

Cranberries contain compounds that, in lab studies, make it harder for some bacteria to stick to the bladder wall. That's where their reputation comes from. The National Institutes of Health, through its center for complementary health ([NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/cranberry)), notes that some research suggests cranberry products may help lower the number of repeat urinary infections in certain people, but that **they have not been shown to treat an infection that has already started**.

A few practical points:

- **Store-bought juice is often loaded with sugar.** Many "cranberry cocktails" have more sugar than fruit in them. If you're watching your blood sugar or your weight, ask before making it a daily habit.
- **Not all capsules are created equal.** The real amount of cranberry varies a lot between brands, and supplements aren't regulated like medicines.
- **It may not mix well with some prescriptions.** NCCIH mentions possible interactions with blood-thinning medicines. If you take one, tell us at your visit that you're using cranberry.
- **It won't stop today's burning.** Even in large amounts, juice won't clear an infection that's already there in time to matter.

## Water: a good companion, a poor treatment

Drinking enough water means you pee more often, which helps wash some bacteria out before they can multiply. Staying well hydrated throughout the day is a smart habit for your urinary tract.

That said, people often fall into two traps:

1. **Thinking that chugging huge amounts of water will cure the infection.** It might ease the feeling a little, but it won't clear an infection that's taken hold.
2. **Forgetting that not everyone should drink extra.** Anyone with heart or kidney conditions who has been told to limit fluids should stick to what their own doctor advised, even with discomfort when urinating.

The sensible approach is to drink water steadily, keep your urine pale and, if symptoms don't let up, come in rather than keep raising the amount.

## Quick table: what can help and what it can't replace

| Home remedy | What it may offer | What it doesn't replace |
|---|---|---|
| Cranberry juice or capsules | Possible support in preventing repeat infections for some people | A urine test or treatment once an infection is present |
| Water throughout the day | More frequent urination that helps flush the bladder | A checkup if burning or urgency keeps going |
| Warm pack on the lower belly | Comfort while you get to the clinic | Knowing whether the infection is moving up |
| Herbal teas (horsetail, chamomile, corn silk) | Fluids and a sense of calm | A diagnosis; some herbs also aren't safe during pregnancy |
| Baking soda or vinegar in water | Nothing proven; baking soda is high in sodium | Anything at all: better to skip it |
| Leftover pills or medicine brought from another country | Risk of masking the infection and helping bacteria become resistant | A proper visit: please don't self-medicate |

## Why "wait and see" can backfire

A mild bladder infection sometimes clears on its own, but there's no way to know at home which one will and which one won't. Left alone, bacteria can travel up to the kidneys, and then you're no longer dealing with a nuisance but with a more serious infection that may need emergency care.

What feels like a UTI isn't always one, either. Some sexually transmitted infections, irritation from scented soaps or a stone in the urinary tract can also cause burning, and each is handled differently. A week of cranberry for something that isn't an infection only delays finding the real cause.

## When to set the remedy aside and come in

A home remedy can keep you company until you get checked, not stand in for it. Come to the clinic if:

- You've had symptoms for a couple of days with no improvement, or things are getting worse, even with water and cranberry.
- This is the second or third time in a few months that the same thing has happened.
- You're a man: urinary infections are less common in men and are always worth a look; at our [men's health](/en/services/salud-hombre) visits the team also checks whether something else is behind it.
- You're pregnant or think you might be: don't wait it out or try herbs on your own.
- Your urine has blood in it, a strong smell that won't go away or a color that worries you.
- You notice discharge, itching or vaginal discomfort along with the burning; in that case a [gynecology](/en/services/ginecologia) check during the same visit makes sense.

## Warning signs: this is not a job for home remedies

If any of these show up, **go to the emergency room or call 911** right away, without a detour through the kitchen:

- A high fever that arrives with shaking chills and a new ache along one side of your back.
- Vomiting so frequent that not even sips of water or an electrolyte drink stay down.
- An older relative who suddenly seems very confused or doesn't recognize family.
- The urge to go with not a single drop coming out.
- Passing out, even briefly.
