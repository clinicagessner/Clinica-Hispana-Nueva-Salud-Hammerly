In November the kitchen never rests: tamales by the dozen, buñuelos in a deep pan of oil and the Thanksgiving turkey in the oven, with more people around the stove and kids running between the grown-ups. It's burn season too: a splash of oil on the forearm, steam bursting from the tamale pot, a skillet handle grabbed without a towel.

Most are small and heal well at home when handled right from the very first minute, which matters most for how the skin does afterward. Here's what to do, which family remedies to leave on the shelf and the signs that a burn needs someone to look at it.

## The first few minutes, step by step

1. **Get away from the heat.** Turn off the burner, push the pot back from the edge and move the person out of the way. If it's oil, don't carry the hot pan to the sink.
2. **Hold the area under cool running water.** Not ice-cold, just cool tap water. Keep it there for a good while; first aid guides usually mention about 10 to 20 minutes, or until the stinging eases a lot. It's the step that helps most and the one people skip most often while they go hunting for a remedy.
3. **Take off rings, bracelets and watches** from the burned hand or arm while the water runs, before the skin swells. If clothing is stuck to the burn, don't pull it off: cool it right through the fabric and let the clinic or the ER remove it.
4. **Cover it loosely with clean gauze**, or a clean cloth that won't shed lint. The point is to shield the skin from air and dirt, not to squeeze it.
5. **Look at the skin** once it's cooled: that tells you whether home care is enough.

If the burn is on a child, cool it with water that's only mildly cool and wrap the rest of their body in a dry towel: little ones lose body heat faster than adults do.

## Family remedies worth skipping

Nearly every family has someone who knows "grandma's remedy." With burns, several of those do more harm than good:

- **Ice or ice water:** extreme cold can injure skin that's already been hurt by heat.
- **Butter, lard or cooking oil:** they trap heat in the skin and dirty the wound.
- **Toothpaste:** it irritates, doesn't truly cool anything and is hard to clean off damaged skin afterward.
- **Egg white, coffee grounds, mustard or flour:** anything from the pantry can carry germs into the burn.
- **Popping blisters:** that bubble protects the new skin forming underneath. If one breaks on its own, don't peel off the loose skin; just cover it with clean gauze.

It's also wise not to smear on creams or ointments left over from some other time without asking first. If you think the burn needs treatment, have it looked at.

## Reading the skin once it's cooled

| How the skin looks | Likely depth | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Red, painful skin with no bubbles, like a sunburn | Superficial burn | Care for it at home, keep it clean and watch for infection |
| Blisters, shiny or weeping skin, a lot of pain | The burn reached deeper into the skin | Worth having it checked at the clinic, especially if it's bigger than a coin |
| White, gray, leathery or charred skin, sometimes with little pain | Deep burn | ER or 911 without waiting |

Pay attention to that last row: a burn that doesn't hurt isn't necessarily a mild one. When heat damages the deep layers, it can also damage the nerves that send pain signals.

## Signs that mean dialing 911 right away

The clinic isn't the right place for these; dial **911** or head straight to an emergency room:

- The burn is **large**: as a rough guide, wider than the palm of the burned person's hand.
- It's on the **face, hands, feet, genitals or over a joint** like a knee, elbow or knuckles.
- The skin looks **white, gray, charred or tough like leather**.
- It's an **electrical burn** from a frayed cord or an appliance that shorted, even if it looks small on the outside.
- It's a **chemical burn** from oven cleaner, bleach or another cleaning product. While help is on the way, rinse with plenty of running water.
- The person burned is a **baby or young child**, or an older adult with thin skin.
- There was **smoke or fire in a closed space** and the person is coughing, sounds hoarse, struggles to breathe or has soot around the nose and mouth.
- Days later, the surrounding skin turns **red and spreads quickly with a fever**, **red streaks** run up the arm or leg, or the person seems confused.

And if oil catches fire in the pan, never throw water on it: turn off the burner and cover the pan with its lid or a metal baking sheet. If the fire doesn't go out, get your family outside and call 911.

## Caring for a small burn at home

- With clean hands, wash the area gently with mild soap and water, without scrubbing, and pat it dry.
- Keep it covered with clean, dry gauze; change it if it gets wet or dirty.
- Don't scratch it or pick at scabs or loose skin.
- Once it has closed, protect the new skin from the sun, since it can darken.

## A kitchen with fewer burns

Most kitchen burns in children come from hot liquids: a cup of coffee, a bowl of instant soup, the water in the tamale pot. A few habits go a long way during these weeks of nonstop cooking:

- Turn pot and pan handles toward the back of the stove and use the rear burners first.
- Mark a "no-go" zone around the stove while you're frying or steaming.
- Don't hold a baby while you cook or drink something hot.
- Lift the tamale pot lid so it sits between your face and the steam, and open microwave popcorn bags away from your face.
- If you're frying a turkey, do it outdoors, away from the house, and never lower it into the oil while it's still frozen.
