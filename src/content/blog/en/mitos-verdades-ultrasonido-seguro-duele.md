Sit in any waiting room long enough and you'll hear a theory about ultrasound. Someone's cousin "was sore for a week from all the pressing." A young man wonders whether he's allowed another scan when he already had one back in May. An expecting mom shows up nervous because an aunt warned her that too many sonograms "heat up the baby." Behind nearly every one of those worries is a half-truth nobody stopped to explain.

Here our clinic's medical team takes the beliefs we hear most often from patients in Spring Branch and sets each one next to what is actually known. This isn't a prep checklist or a guide to picking the right scan. It's meant to help you walk in calm, knowing what the screen can tell you and what it can't.

## Myth 1: "Ultrasound gives off radiation, just a little"

**Fact:** ultrasound uses no radiation at all. What it relies on is sound, at a pitch no person can hear. The handheld device sends those waves into the body, they bounce off organs and come back as a picture. That's why our [ultrasound page](/en/services/ultrasonido) notes the scan involves no radiation and no needles.

That doesn't make it a toy. The waves do jiggle tissue slightly and can warm it a tiny bit. For that reason U.S. health agencies ask that scans be done for a medical reason, for as long as the exam requires and no longer. Think of it as good practice, not a warning sign.

## Myth 2: "It's definitely going to hurt"

**Fact:** for most people, the worst part is how cold the gel feels. The device glides across the skin and now and then presses a little to get a better look at an organ. You may be asked to hold your breath for a few seconds or roll onto your side.

There are two moments when you may feel something:

- **When the area is already tender.** If you came in because your right side aches after fatty meals, pressure over the gallbladder will register. Speak up: the angle and pressure can be adjusted.
- **When the scan calls for a full bladder.** During a pelvic ultrasound, holding it is the hardest part. That discomfort comes from your bladder, not the device, and it ends the minute you're done and head to the restroom.

If something genuinely bothers you, don't stay quiet to be polite. The exam can pause.

## Myth 3: "You're only allowed so many scans a year"

**Fact:** there's no yearly cap. Imaging that uses radiation has a dose that adds up over time and needs tracking; ultrasound leaves nothing behind in the body. What decides whether you get another one is the medical reason for it.

It's common for a patient to need one scan now and another a few months later, say to check whether an ovarian cyst has grown or shrunk, or to follow a baby's growth through pregnancy. Repeating it in those cases is how comparisons get made. What makes no sense is a weekly "just in case" scan nobody ordered: it adds no information and it's no substitute for a visit.

## Myth 4: "The gel is medicine" (or "the gel stains and is bad for you")

**Fact:** the gel doesn't treat anything. Its only job is to push out the air between the device and your skin, because sound travels poorly through air and without gel the image turns fuzzy. It's clear and water-based, and you wipe it off with a paper towel afterward. It usually doesn't stain clothes.

If your skin is very sensitive or you once broke out from a similar product, mention it before the scan starts.

## Myth 5: "Every pregnancy scan harms the baby"

**Fact:** ultrasound is the imaging test used most during pregnancy precisely because it has no radiation, and when it's done for a medical reason it hasn't been shown to harm the baby. It confirms the pregnancy is on track and lets the team follow the baby's growth.

One nuance matters, though. The FDA advises against "keepsake" scans done without a medical reason and long sessions just to record video. Not because harm has been proven, but because there's no reason to expose a baby for longer than needed.

Two more truths worth repeating:

- A sonogram doesn't replace prenatal care. Your visits, lab work and blood pressure checks still matter.
- If you've had a positive [pregnancy test](/en/services/prueba-embarazo) and you notice heavy bleeding, sharp belly pain or fainting, don't wait for an appointment: go to the ER or call 911.

And yes, you can bring your partner or a relative to watch the baby on the screen with you; if the room is small, only one person may be able to come in.

## Myth 6: "Ultrasound sees everything"

**Fact:** it's an excellent tool with clear limits. Sound doesn't pass well through air or bone, which plays out like this:

- **Intestinal gas** can block part of the abdomen, which is one reason fasting is sometimes requested.
- **Bones** and **lungs** barely show up with this method.
- When there's more tissue around the belly, sound has farther to travel and the picture can lose sharpness.
- A scan shows **shape, size and whether something is fluid or solid**, but it often can't say exactly what a spot is.

So our clinic's medical team reads the images alongside your symptoms, and sometimes the next step is [blood work](/en/services/examenes-sangre) or a different test. If a finding calls for it, the team guides the referral to a specialist. Nobody honest will tell you a sonogram "rules out anything."

## Myth 7: "A normal result means nothing is wrong"

**Fact:** a normal scan is good news because it helps rule out several causes. It just can't explain every symptom. Some problems leave no trace on the image, such as certain stomach or bowel issues, or pain that comes from muscle.

If the pain stays, shifts or gets worse after your scan, come back. The ultrasound didn't "get it wrong"; the answer simply lies somewhere else.

## Myth 8: "If they order an ultrasound, it must be serious"

**Fact:** most scans are ordered to answer a question, not to confirm a serious illness. A woman with very heavy periods, a man with discomfort after eating, a pregnant patient at a routine follow-up: in each case the scan gives a calm look at what hands alone can't feel.

Ordering an image is part of doing things right, not bad news delivered early.

## Myths and facts at a glance

| What people say | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| It uses radiation | It uses sound waves; no radiation, no needles |
| It hurts | Usually just some pressure and cold gel |
| You can only have a few | It's repeated whenever there's a medical reason |
| The gel is a treatment | It only removes air so the image comes through |
| It harms the baby | With a medical reason, it's the go-to scan in pregnancy |
| It sees everything | Air, gas and bone set its limits |

## When not to wait for a scan

Some symptoms call for immediate care, not a scheduled study. If you have severe belly pain, a high fever along with abdominal pain, or vomiting that won't stop, go to the ER or call 911. Imaging can happen later, once you're stable.
